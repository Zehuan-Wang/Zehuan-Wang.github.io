## Introduction

I was really excited to start my PhD at Tongji University this year. However, weekly meetings soon became one of the things I found most stressful. During my first month, I often struggled to fall asleep the night before a meeting, worrying that I hadn't done enough work that week or that my supervisor would be disappointed with my progress.

Over time, I also noticed a different problem among students I had encountered throughout my undergraduate, master's, and PhD studies. Some students were actually quite productive but struggled to communicate their work effectively during group meetings. They would walk their supervisors through everything they had done chronologically, without highlighting the key findings, decisions, or challenges. Others couldn't clearly articulate the difficulties they were facing, leaving their supervisors with an incomplete understanding of the situation.

As a result, an entire hour could pass without addressing the most critical issue. The student would leave the meeting without a clear direction, only to find themselves stuck at the same point the following week.

Through repeated experience, I gradually developed my own approach to weekly meetings. I came to realize that a weekly meeting should be more than an opportunity to demonstrate how much work you've completed. It should be a space to exchange ideas, discuss challenges, seek guidance, and ultimately become a better researcher.

In this post, I'd like to share some reflections and practical tips that have helped me make weekly meetings more productive. Hopefully, they'll help you approach your next meeting with a little less anxiety and a lot more confidence.

<Callout>
**TL;DR:** A productive weekly meeting synchronizes progress, turns findings into better decisions, and helps you grow into an independent researcher — it is not a weekly performance review.
</Callout>


## 1. Why Do We Struggle with Weekly Meetings?

### 1.1 The Pressure of Being Evaluated

One reason weekly meetings can be stressful is that we tend to treat them as a form of weekly performance evaluation.

Especially during the early stages of a PhD, it's easy to worry about whether we've made enough progress, whether our work is substantial enough to present, or whether our supervisor will be satisfied with what we've accomplished.

This mindset can make us feel that we need to demonstrate tangible results every single week. We may even feel uncomfortable bringing up unfinished work, uncertain ideas, or experiments that didn't go as expected.

However, research rarely progresses in a perfectly linear way. Spending a week reviewing literature and realizing that a research direction is not worth pursuing, identifying a flaw in an experimental design, or ruling out several infeasible approaches can all represent meaningful research progress.

Sometimes, understanding what doesn't work is just as important as finding out what does.

<Callout>
Not every productive week leads to a tangible outcome.
</Callout>

When we focus too much on proving our productivity, we risk overlooking what a weekly meeting is actually supposed to help us achieve: making better research decisions.

### 1.2 Being Busy Doesn't Mean Communicating Effectively

Another common issue is that students often confuse reporting their activities with communicating their research progress.

I've encountered students who put considerable effort into their research but struggled to convey what they had actually achieved during group meetings. Their presentations often followed a chronological structure, going through every detail: what they read, what they implemented, what experiments they ran, and what they did next.

While this provides a record of their activities, it doesn't necessarily help their supervisors understand the bigger picture.

More importantly, when students encounter difficulties, they sometimes describe the symptoms without explaining the underlying problem. Their supervisors may therefore misunderstand the situation or spend valuable meeting time trying to figure out what is actually going on.

The consequence is frustrating: an entire meeting can pass without resolving the issue that matters most, leaving the student with little clarity about how to move forward.

Ultimately, the problem isn't necessarily a lack of effort. It's often a lack of effective communication: knowing what matters, how to organize information, and how to articulate your own thinking.


## 2. What Is a Weekly Meeting Really For?

If weekly meetings are not simply about proving how much work we've done, what should we actually expect from them?

I believe a productive weekly meeting serves three purposes: synchronizing progress, discussing research questions, and developing ourselves as independent researchers.

### 2.1 Progress Synchronization: Build a Shared Context

The first purpose of a weekly meeting is to keep your supervisor informed about your research progress and, more importantly, ensure that both of you share an accurate understanding of where the research currently stands.

Your supervisor is unlikely to be involved in every detail of your day-to-day work. Between two meetings, you may have read new papers, revised your experimental design, encountered unexpected results, or even reconsidered some of your original assumptions.

Without regular updates, your supervisor's understanding of your research may gradually become outdated or incomplete.

This can lead to two problems. First, your supervisor might offer suggestions based on an incomplete understanding of the current situation. Second, potential issues may go unnoticed until they become much harder or more expensive to address.

Therefore, a weekly meeting provides an opportunity to establish and maintain a shared context. Your supervisor should have a clear understanding of:

- **Current Status:** Where are you in your research?
- **Recent Developments:** What important changes or findings have emerged?
- **Current Challenges:** What are the main obstacles you are facing?
- **Next Milestones:** What are you aiming to achieve next?

More importantly, regular synchronization allows your supervisor to identify potential issues early and provide timely guidance before they significantly affect your research progress.

### 2.2 Research Discussion: Turn Progress into Better Decisions

The second purpose is to discuss preliminary findings, research assumptions, and challenges that require further thought.

Research rarely follows a perfectly predictable path. You may encounter unexpected experimental results, discover contradictions in the literature, or find that an initially promising approach doesn't work as expected.

These situations are valuable opportunities for discussion.

Your supervisor brings years of research experience, a broader understanding of the field, and perspectives that you may not have considered. Discussing your findings and challenges with them can help you identify blind spots, reconsider assumptions, and explore alternative approaches.

However, a productive discussion should not be a one-way process in which you simply ask questions and wait for answers. You should bring your own interpretations, hypotheses, and potential solutions to the table.

The goal is not necessarily to reach a definitive conclusion during every meeting, but to make better-informed research decisions and gain clarity on how to move forward.

### 2.3 Researcher Development: Become an Independent Researcher

Beyond making progress on your current project, weekly meetings are also opportunities to develop yourself as a researcher.

During a PhD, you are not just expected to complete a research project. You are also learning how to identify meaningful research questions, evaluate existing approaches, design rigorous studies, interpret evidence, and communicate research contributions.

Your supervisor's feedback can help you develop these capabilities over time.

For example, when discussing an experimental design, you might initially focus on whether an experiment can be implemented. Your supervisor may instead challenge you to consider whether the experiment can actually answer your research question.

Similarly, when reviewing your findings, you might focus on whether the results are statistically significant, while your supervisor encourages you to think more deeply about their theoretical implications.

These discussions gradually shape how you approach research problems.

Ultimately, the goal is not to become increasingly dependent on your supervisor for every decision. It is to develop the ability to make sound research judgments independently.

<Callout>
A good weekly meeting should not only move your research forward, but also help you become a better researcher.
</Callout>


## 3. How to Make Your Weekly Meeting Productive?

Understanding the purpose of weekly meetings is one thing; making them productive is another.

Over time, I've found that a productive weekly meeting requires more than just preparing a few slides. It's about knowing what to communicate, how to present your thinking, and how to turn discussions into actionable next steps.

Here are four practices that have helped me make better use of weekly meetings.

### 3.1 Start with a Recap: Connect the Dots

At the beginning of each meeting, I recommend briefly revisiting what was discussed last time before jumping into this week's progress.

Your supervisor may be working with multiple students and projects, so don't assume they remember every detail of your previous discussion.

A short recap can help bring both sides back to the same context. Focus on three things:

- **Key Takeaways:** What were the main conclusions or suggestions from the previous meeting?
- **Pending Issues:** What questions or challenges remain unresolved?
- **Action Items:** What did you agree to work on, and what progress have you made?

You don't need to repeat the entire previous presentation. The goal is simply to reconnect the dots and provide a smooth transition into the current discussion.

### 3.2 Start with the Goal, Not the To-do List

One of the most important lessons I've learned is that your weekly update should be organized around the core objective of your current research stage, rather than simply listing everything you've done.

The nature of research changes as a project progresses, and so should the focus of your updates.

For example:

- **Literature Review:** If your current goal is to understand the state of the art, prioritize highly relevant papers, emerging research gaps, and insights that may influence your research direction. There is little value in reporting every paper you've read without explaining what you've learned from them.
- **Research Design:** If you're establishing your research approach, focus on your research questions, methodological choices, and the rationale behind them.
- **Prototype Development:** If your goal is to realize certain design considerations, explain the reasoning behind your design decisions, the trade-offs you've made, and any challenges preventing you from moving forward.
- **Experimentation:** If you're validating a research hypothesis, focus on the key findings, unexpected observations, and what they might imply for your original assumptions.
- **Analysis & Writing:** Focus on your key findings, how you interpret them, any contradictory evidence, and questions that remain unresolved.

In other words, always ask yourself: What is the most important thing I am trying to achieve at this stage, and which parts of this week's work contribute to that goal?

Another useful principle is to distinguish between expected progress and unexpected developments.

Your supervisor is an experienced researcher and likely has a reasonable understanding of what normal progress should look like. You don't need to spend too much time explaining every routine task that went as expected.

Instead, give a brief overview of routine progress and dedicate more time to:

- **Deviations:** What didn't go as expected, and why?
- **Discoveries:** Did you encounter any unexpected but potentially valuable findings?
- **Decisions:** What important decisions did you make, and what was your reasoning?
- **Challenges:** What is preventing you from moving forward?

For example, if you ran ten experiments and eight of them showed no significant improvement, you probably don't need to walk your supervisor through all ten experiments individually. Summarize the overall results, then focus on the two unexpected observations and what they might tell you.

<Callout>
Report progress briefly, but discuss deviations, discoveries, and decisions in depth.
</Callout>

### 3.3 Don't Just Bring Problems. Bring Your Thinking.

Sooner or later, you'll encounter problems that you cannot easily solve on your own. That's perfectly normal, and seeking your supervisor's advice is one of the reasons weekly meetings exist.

However, there's a difference between asking your supervisor to solve a problem for you and bringing a problem to them with your own analysis.

Before presenting a challenge, try to prepare five things:

1. **Context — What is the problem, and why does it matter?** Explain where the problem occurs and how it affects your current research objectives. Give your supervisor enough background to understand the situation.
2. **Diagnosis — What might have caused it?** Don't just describe what happened. Think about the possible underlying causes and whether there are multiple explanations.
3. **Exploration — What have you already tried?** Explain the approaches you've explored, why you chose them, and what happened. Even unsuccessful attempts can provide useful information.
4. **Alternatives — What are the possible solutions?** Propose a few candidate solutions and discuss their respective advantages, disadvantages, and potential trade-offs.
5. **Seeking Advice — What exactly do you need help with?** Be specific about what you want your supervisor to help you decide. Is it the validity of your diagnosis, the feasibility of a particular approach, or which direction is worth pursuing?

You don't need to have the answer before asking for help. But you should demonstrate that you've made a genuine effort to understand the problem.

Your supervisor is there to guide you, not to do your thinking for you.

A productive discussion happens when both sides bring their knowledge and perspectives to the table.

### 3.4 Wrap Up and Plan Ahead

Before ending the meeting, take a few minutes to summarize the discussion and make sure both sides are aligned on what happens next.

I usually find it helpful to focus on two things:

1. **Summarize Key Takeaways:** Briefly recap the important conclusions, suggestions, and decisions made during the meeting. If some questions remain unresolved, make sure they are explicitly acknowledged rather than forgotten.
2. **Confirm Next Steps:** Discuss and confirm what you should work on before the next meeting. Try to make your next steps as concrete as possible:
   - **Priorities:** What should you focus on first?
   - **Tasks:** What needs to be completed?
   - **Expected Outcomes:** What results or deliverables are you aiming for?
   - **Potential Obstacles:** Are there any dependencies or challenges that might affect your plans?

This helps avoid misunderstandings and gives you a clear starting point for the following week.

One additional point: don't let weekly meetings become the only channel through which you communicate with your supervisor.

If you can already anticipate potential challenges, you may bring them up before ending the meeting and seek advice in advance. However, this doesn't always have to happen during the meeting.

More importantly, if you encounter an issue between two meetings that could significantly delay your research, communicate it to your supervisor as soon as reasonably possible. Don't wait until the next scheduled meeting just because that's when you're supposed to discuss research.

<Callout>
A weekly meeting should provide a regular rhythm for your research, not become a bottleneck that holds it back.
</Callout>


## Conclusion: From Reporting Work to Managing Your Own Research

Looking back, one of the biggest changes in how I approach weekly meetings is that I no longer see them as an opportunity to prove how much work I've done.

Instead, I see them as an opportunity to make better research decisions, learn from my supervisor's experience, and gradually develop my own research capabilities.

A productive weekly meeting is not necessarily one where you present impressive results or have every question answered. Sometimes, the most valuable discussions come from an unexpected finding, a failed experiment, or a question that makes you reconsider your original assumptions.

What matters is whether you can communicate your thinking clearly, take ownership of the problems you encounter, and turn discussions into meaningful next steps.

More importantly, learning how to make your weekly meetings productive is also part of learning how to manage your own research. As a PhD student, you are gradually moving from someone who follows research instructions to someone who can identify problems, make informed decisions, and take responsibility for their research direction.

So, the next time you prepare for a weekly meeting, don't just ask yourself:

> What have I done this week?

Try asking:

> What have I learned? What am I struggling with? What decisions do I need to make? And how can this meeting help me move forward?

<Callout>
A weekly meeting is not about proving that you're a good PhD student. It's about learning how to become a better researcher.
</Callout>
